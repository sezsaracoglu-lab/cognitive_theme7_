🧵 Synthesis

Thesis: solving a problem, finding an analogy and having a creative idea all depend on the same move: getting past the first representation your mind offers. Well-structured problems can be solved by stepping through a defined problem space with algorithms or heuristics like means-ends analysis and hill climbing. Ill-structured and insight problems fail at the representation stage: mental sets, functional fixedness and surface features keep us inside a familiar frame (Luchins; Gick & Holyoak). Analogies work when we see structure rather than surface, which comparison, enactment and asking ‘why’ encourage; insight is the moment the representation is restructured (Metcalfe & Wiebe). Creativity research makes the mechanism explicit: our intuitive System 1 serves up dominant ideas, and inhibitory control is needed to push them aside so System 2 can expand into original territory (Cassotti et al.; Camarda et al., 2018). Where the science is still debated: whether disinhibition can also help creativity (Radel et al., 2015), whether insight is truly special or gradual, and how well lab analogies reflect real-world problem solving.

Readings: Matlin, Cognition (pp. 380–387) · Robinson-Riegler, Cognitive Psychology (pp. 512–520) · Sternberg, Sternberg & Mio, Cognitive Psychology (pp. 454–465) · Camarda et al. (2018), Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 12(3), 351–358.

🧩 Kinds of problems

❓ The question this lecture answers
A1
Which types of problems exist?

Short answer: Well-structured problems (a clear path through a defined problem space, e.g. water jars, Tower of Hanoi, algebra; many are transformation problems) and ill-structured problems (no clear path, e.g. the two-string problem). Problems can also be isomorphs: same structure, different surface.

C1
Which types of problems exist? (Sternberg)

Short answer: Well-structured vs ill-structured problems; many ill-structured problems are insight problems, which can only be solved by restructuring the representation (hat rack, minister, socks).

  • 🧭 A problem = you are in an initial state, want to reach a goal state, and obstacles are in the way (Matlin).
  • ✅ Well-structured problems have a clear path to the solution (a well-defined problem space): water jars, Tower of Hanoi, algebra, Elves-and-Goblins. Many are transformation problems: a series of moves from start to goal (Robinson-Riegler).
  • 🌫️ Ill-structured problems have no clear, readily available path; the hard part is building a good representation. Domain knowledge and justification skills help, since several representations and solutions are possible (Shin, Jonassen & McGee, 2003).
  • 💡 Many ill-structured problems are insight problems: you must restructure how you see the problem (two-string, hat rack, minister, socks).
  • 🔁 Isomorphs share the same underlying structure with different surface details (radiation ≈ fortress; mutilated checkerboard ≈ country-dance problem).
🪝 Memory hook: A well-structured problem is a train track; an ill-structured problem is an open field in fog.

🪜 The steps of problem solving

❓ The question this lecture answers
A2
What are the different steps in problem solving?

Short answer: Identify the initial and goal state, understand the problem by building a mental representation (symbols, lists, matrices, trees, diagrams, images), choose a strategy, work past obstacles, and monitor and evaluate the solution.

  • 🚩 Start: identify the initial state, the goal state and the obstacles in between (Matlin).
  • 🧠 Understand the problem = build an accurate mental representation: symbols, lists, matrices, hierarchical trees, diagrams or visual images.
  • 👀 Attention matters: it is limited, competing thoughts divide it, and you must focus on the relevant part of the problem.
  • 🧭 Then pick a strategy (algorithm or heuristic: next lessons), work through obstacles, monitor progress and evaluate the solution.
  • 🤸 Embodied & situated cognition: cues from the environment and your own body shape the representation (swinging arms helps the two-string problem).
🪝 Memory hook: Good problem solving is 80% map-making: you can't navigate a city you've drawn wrongly.
★ KEY INSIGHT · The most common place to get stuck is the representation step: people who fail the two-string problem usually impose a constraint (‘I must walk to the string’) at step 2 and never recover (Sternberg).
Tap to open ▸
First figure out where you are and where you want to be. Then draw yourself a picture or a list of the problem, because a bad picture makes it impossible. Only then choose how to attack it, keep checking whether you're getting closer, and check your answer at the end.

🧮 Algorithms vs heuristics

❓ The question this lecture answers
A3
Which well-known problem-solving strategies are there? (part 1)

Short answer: Algorithms (always give a solution, e.g. exhaustive search, but are slow) and heuristics (fast rules of thumb that are not guaranteed). The three classic heuristics are analogy, means-ends analysis and hill climbing.

  • 🐢 An algorithm always produces a solution, but can be very slow. Example: exhaustive search of every arrangement of SIMULTANEOUSLY (more than 87 billion).
  • 🐇 A heuristic is a general rule that is usually correct; it explores only promising alternatives (try pronounceable starts: LE, LU, SI).
  • ⚠️ Heuristics are not guaranteed: rejecting rare starts like LY makes you miss LYMPHOID (IPMHYLOD).
  • 🌍 We solve most everyday problems with heuristics; researchers study heuristics more than algorithms. The big three: analogy, means-ends, hill-climbing.
  • ⚖️ Dual process: heuristics are not simply System 1 and algorithms not simply System 2; both can be either (Robinson-Riegler).
🪝 Memory hook: An algorithm is checking every key on the key ring; a heuristic is trying the one that looks like it fits the lock.
★ KEY INSIGHT · The trade-off is speed vs certainty: heuristics are faster but can miss the correct solution.
Tap to open ▸
You can solve a puzzle by trying absolutely everything: that always works but can take years. Or you use a smart shortcut that usually works much faster but can occasionally lead you to miss the answer. In daily life we nearly always use the shortcuts.

🎯 Learning-objective lecture 4 of 14 · Strategies

🎯 Means-ends analysis and hill climbing

❓ The question this lecture answers
A3
Which well-known problem-solving strategies are there? (part 2)

Short answer: Means-ends analysis: split the problem into sub-goals and reduce the difference to each (simulated by the General Problem Solver). Hill climbing: always take the step that seems closest to the goal, which can miss better detours.

  • 🎯 Means-ends heuristic: (1) divide the problem into sub-problems, (2) reduce the difference between current state and goal for each. One of the most effective and flexible strategies.
  • 📎 Example: a skirt hem comes loose 10 minutes before a talk → sub-goals ‘find a hem-fixer’ + ‘locate it’ → a stapler (Matlin).
  • ⏸️ People pause before each sub-problem in Elves & Goblins (Greeno, 1974), and resist moves that temporarily increase the distance to the goal (step 6 goes backwards).
  • 💻 General Problem Solver (Newell & Simon, 1972): a computer simulation of means-ends analysis built from think-aloud protocols; later dropped because it was not general enough. Anderson’s ACT-R models led to cognitive tutors.
  • ⛰️ Hill climbing: at every choice point take the step that seems to lead most directly to the goal; useful with little information, but favours short-term goals and can lead to dead ends.
🪝 Memory hook: Means-ends is a to-do list of mini-goals; hill climbing is walking towards the GPS dot without checking the road map.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Sometimes the only way forward is to move temporarily backward, and people find that very hard.
Tap to open ▸
With means-ends you split a big goal into small goals and close each gap. With hill climbing you always take the step that looks closest right now, which works until a wall is in the way and you'd actually need to step back first.

🔁 Analogies: transfer from a solved problem

❓ The question this lecture answers
A4
How can analogies be used to effectively solve problems? (part 1)

Short answer: Use the solution of a solved source problem for a new target problem: notice the link, map the elements, and build a schema. People rarely notice analogies on their own (about 20%), but a hint (about 75%) or comparing two analogues (schema induction) makes transfer work.

  • 🔁 Analogy approach: use the solution of a similar earlier problem (the source) to solve a new one (the target). Engineers made on average 102 analogies in 9 hours (Christensen & Schunn, 2007); the Wright brothers copied birds’ wing tips.
  • ☢️ Radiation problem (Duncker, 1945): destroy a tumour without harming tissue → many weak rays from different angles that converge.
  • 📉 Without a source story only ~10% find convergence; after memorising the fortress story 30%; so only about 20% spontaneously notice the analogy (Gick & Holyoak, 1980, Robinson-Riegler). With a hint to use the story: about 75%.
  • 📚 Giving a statement of the principle or a diagram did not help; giving two analogous stories to compare did, through schema induction (Gick & Holyoak, 1983).
  • 🧩 Three steps: notice the relationship, map the elements (tumour = fortress), build the schema (Novick & Holyoak, 1991). Noticing is the main bottleneck: a failure of memory retrieval.
🪝 Memory hook: An analogy is a key cut for one door that fits another; the hard part is remembering you have the key.
★ KEY INSIGHT · People can use analogies very well (75% with a hint) but rarely notice them on their own: the bottleneck is retrieval, not reasoning.

🎭 Making analogies work

❓ The question this lecture answers
A4
How can analogies be used to effectively solve problems? (part 2)

Short answer: Focus on structural rather than surface features: practise several similar problems, sort problems by structure, ask why examples work, compare examples, and enact the source (52% vs 19% spontaneous transfer).

  • 🎨 Surface features = specific elements (what it looks like); structural features = underlying relationships. People focus on surface features and miss structural analogies; transparency = seeing analogies where only content is similar.
  • 🗣️ Thinking aloud pushes people to surface analogies (3.8 vs 1.0) and away from structural ones (Lane & Schooler, 2004).
  • 🌍 In real life people mostly generate structural analogies (Blanchette & Dunbar, 2000; Dunbar, 1995); listening rather than reading helps retrieve structurally similar proverbs (Markman, Taylor & Gentner, 2007).
  • 🧱 Enacting the source story with blocks: 52% solved the radiation problem spontaneously vs 27% (sketch) and 19% (verbal) (Catrambone, Craig & Nersessian, 2006).
  • ✅ Also helps: practise several structurally similar problems, sort problems by structure (Quilici & Mayer, 2002), ask why examples work (Chi et al., 1989). Rutgers solved application tracking by looking at FedEx package tracking (structure), not other universities (surface).
🪝 Memory hook: Don’t judge a problem by its cover story.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Analogies transfer through structure, but memory is cued by surface: good problem solvers learn to look past the cover story.

🧠 Forms of thinking

❓ The question this lecture answers
C2
Which forms of thinking are there?

Short answer: Reproductive thinking (re-using existing associations) vs productive thinking (insight beyond existing associations; Wertheimer), and intuitive (System 1) vs analytic (System 2) thinking. Which works best depends on experience (Pretz, 2008).

  • 🔁 Reproductive thinking: based on existing associations, what you already know (Wertheimer).
  • ✨ Productive thinking: insight that goes beyond existing associations; you see the problem in a new light (Wertheimer, 1945/1959). Gestaltists: perceive the problem as a whole.
  • 🐒 Köhler (1927): the chimpanzee Sultan suddenly stacked boxes to reach bananas: insight as a special, non-linear process.
  • ⚡ Intuitive (System 1) vs 🧠 analytic (System 2) thinking. Pretz (2008): first-year students did better on college problems with intuition; juniors did better with analysis (College Student Tacit Knowledge Inventory).
  • 🔀 In creativity: generative / conceptual expansion (System 2) vs fixated, heuristic ideas (System 1) (Cassotti et al., 2016a).
🪝 Memory hook: Reproductive = reheating yesterday’s recipe; productive = inventing a new dish.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Neither mode is always better: it depends on the problem and on your experience (Pretz, 2008).
Tap to open ▸
Sometimes you solve a problem by doing what worked before; sometimes you need a completely new way of looking at it. Whether gut feeling or careful analysis works better depends on how much you know about the area.

🫙 Obstacles: mental set and fixedness

❓ The question this lecture answers
C3
How do people generally solve problems?

Short answer: Mostly by applying what worked before: they use mental sets and transfer knowledge from earlier problems. This gives positive transfer when the problems match, but leads to entrenchment, functional fixedness and negative transfer when they don’t (Luchins: 64–83% kept using the long formula).

  • 🫙 Mental set / entrenchment: an existing model of a problem or procedure that you fixate on, even when it doesn’t fit.
  • 💧 Luchins (1942) water jars: after problems 1–6 (B − A − 2C), 64–83% kept using the long formula on 7–11 vs only 1–5% of controls.
  • 🔧 Functional fixedness: can’t see a new use for a familiar object (C-clamp as hat holder; coat hanger to open a car).
  • 👥 Stereotypes are a social mental set: overgeneralising from limited observations limits thinking (awareness develops between ages 6 and 10).
  • ↔️ Transfer = carry-over of knowledge: negative (earlier problem makes a new one harder) or positive (easier).
🪝 Memory hook: A mental set is a well-worn path in the snow: easy to follow, hard to leave.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Experience is double-edged: it gives you positive transfer and entrenchment at the same time.
Tap to open ▸
Once a trick works a few times, your brain keeps using it, even when there’s a much easier way. The same happens with objects (‘a clamp is for clamping’) and with people (stereotypes).

📦 Thinking outside the box

❓ The question this lecture answers
C4
How do we think outside of the box?

Short answer: By escaping fixation: incubation, questioning self-imposed constraints and restructuring the problem, deliberately looking for structural analogies, using the body (enactment), and inhibiting the first, obvious ideas.

  • 🛁 Incubation: put the problem aside without consciously thinking about it; it reduces negative transfer. Evidence is mixed (Baron, 1988), but a meta-analysis shows it works best after longer preparation, with undemanding filler tasks, and for divergent tasks (Sio & Ormerod, 2009).
  • 🔍 Look for structure, not content: we usually only find analogies if we explicitly look for them (Chi et al., 1989).
  • 🔄 Restructure the representation: question the constraints you added yourself (two-string: bring the string to you).
  • 🤸 Use your body and environment: arm-swinging ‘exercise breaks’ help the two-string problem; enactment helps analogies.
  • 🚦 For creative ideas: inhibit the first, dominant ideas to escape fixation (Camarda et al., 2018).
🪝 Memory hook: To get out of the box you first have to notice the walls.
★ KEY INSIGHT · ‘Outside the box’ = escaping fixation: dropping a representation, use or idea that came to mind first.
Tap to open ▸
When you’re stuck, the problem is usually an invisible rule you’ve set yourself. Take a break, look for a problem with the same structure, physically play with it, and consciously push away your first obvious idea.

💡 What is insight?

❓ The question this lecture answers
C5
What is insight?

Short answer: A distinctive and sometimes seemingly sudden understanding of a problem or strategy, reached by restructuring the representation. Unlike routine problems, people don’t feel themselves getting closer (Metcalfe & Wiebe), though insight can also build up gradually and benefit from sleep.

  • 💡 Insight: a distinctive and sometimes seemingly sudden understanding of a problem or strategy, often by reconceptualising it and combining old and new information.
  • 🏛️ Gestalt view: insight is a special process, different from linear information processing (Wertheimer; Köhler).
  • 🌡️ Neo-Gestalt (Metcalfe, 1986; Metcalfe & Wiebe, 1987): people predict success on routine but not insight problems; ‘warmth’ rises gradually for algebra but stays flat until just before an insight.
  • 🐢 But insights need not be sudden; they can be gradual (Smith, 1995a), follow much prior work, and sleep raises the chance of insight (Wagner et al., 2004).
  • 🧠 Brain: right anterior superior temporal gyrus and an EEG burst at insight (Jung-Beeman et al., 2004); right hippocampus (Luo & Niki, 2003); frontal vs occipital activity before seeing a problem predicts insight (Kounios et al., 2006).
🪝 Memory hook: Insight is the lights switching on in a room you’ve been fumbling around in.

❓ The question this lecture answers
D5
How are dual process theory and creativity related?

Short answer: In the dual-process model of creativity, System 1 automatically produces dominant, fixated ideas; inhibitory control must suppress them so System 2 can expand into original ideas (Cassotti et al., 2016a).

  • ⚡ System 1: fast, intuitive, heuristic, automatic → dominant, common ideas → fixation.
  • 🧠 System 2: slow, deliberate, analytic → generative reasoning, conceptual expansion, analogical thinking.

  • 🔀 But the mapping is not neat: heuristics and algorithms can each be System 1 or 2 (Robinson-Riegler), and intuition helps novices (Pretz, 2008).

Our fast brain always serves up the obvious idea first. To be creative you have to say ‘no’ to that idea and then deliberately think further. That’s why being creative takes effort and control, not just letting go.

🩺 Triage: how to recognise and attack it
Symptom‘discuss the steps … through means-end analysis’
Diagnosismeans-ends heuristic (Matlin; Robinson-Riegler; Newell & Simon)
Treatmentstate goal, find the biggest difference, set sub-goals, reduce each difference, repeat; mention backward moves
  • Goal: the whole tower on rod C. Find the biggest difference: the largest disk.
  • Sub-goal: clear the 3 smaller disks onto B, so the largest can move.
  • Each sub-goal splits into smaller sub-goals: the same pattern repeats.
  • Sometimes a move seems to go away from the goal; means-ends accepts that.
  • Minimum for 4 disks: 2⁴ − 1 = 15 moves.
🪝 Memory hook: Means-end analysis on Hanoi: ‘to move the big one I must first move the small ones out of the way’, again and again.
Tap to open ▸

Means-end analysis = (1) divide the problem into sub-problems and (2) reduce the difference between the current state and the goal state for each (Matlin). Robinson-Riegler: break a transformation problem into sub-goals; each one achieved moves you closer to the goal.

  • Step 1: define initial and goal state. Initial: all 4 disks on rod A. Goal: all 4 on rod C, in the same order.
  • Step 2: find the most important difference. The largest disk is not on C. Everything else depends on this, because it must go to the bottom.
  • Step 3: set a sub-goal to remove that difference. ‘Move the largest disk to C’. That move is blocked by the three smaller disks, which creates a new sub-goal: ‘move the 3-disk tower to B’.
  • Step 4: apply the same analysis to each sub-goal (recursively). To move 3 disks to B, first move 2 disks to C, and so on. Each sub-goal is a smaller version of the same problem.
  • Step 5: execute, check the difference, choose the next sub-goal. Once the largest disk is on C, the new goal is to move the 3-disk tower from B onto C.

Watch out: means-end analysis sometimes requires moves that seem to increase the distance to the goal (putting disks on B, the ‘wrong’ rod). People resist such detours (Matlin; Elves & Goblins). Greeno (1974) showed people pause when they start a new sub-problem. The fewest possible moves is 2⁴ − 1 = 15.

In general: System 1 is fast, intuitive and heuristic. In idea generation it automatically produces the most accessible, common ideas (the ‘path of least resistance’), which causes fixation (slow the fall, protect the egg). System 2 is slow and deliberate and enables generative reasoning: conceptual expansion and analogical thinking. To be original you must first inhibit System 1’s dominant ideas, then explore with System 2 (Cassotti et al., 2016a).

Experiment 1: the incongruent Stroop uses up inhibitory control (you must suppress reading the word). It shows that a second task without inhibition does not reduce creativity.

Thesis: solving a problem, finding an analogy and having a creative idea all depend on the same move: getting past the first representation your mind offers. Well-structured problems can be solved by stepping through a defined problem space with algorithms or heuristics like means-ends analysis and hill climbing. Ill-structured and insight problems fail at the representation stage: mental sets, functional fixedness and surface features keep us inside a familiar frame (Luchins; Gick & Holyoak). Analogies work when we see structure rather than surface, which comparison, enactment and asking ‘why’ encourage; insight is the moment the representation is restructured (Metcalfe & Wiebe). Creativity research makes the mechanism explicit: our intuitive System 1 serves up dominant ideas, and inhibitory control is needed to push them aside so System 2 can expand into original territory (Cassotti et al.; Camarda et al., 2018). Where the science is still debated: whether disinhibition can also help creativity (Radel et al., 2015), whether insight is truly special or gradual, and how well lab analogies reflect real-world problem solving.
